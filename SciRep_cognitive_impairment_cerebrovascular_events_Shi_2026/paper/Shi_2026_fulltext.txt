Baseline cognitive impairment predicts cerebrovascular
events in stroke-free chinese adults
Zhaobo Shi · Anxin Wang · Xia Meng · Xue Tian · Yong Sun · Jing Li · Xiaoli Zhang ·
Xinsheng Han

Received: 21 July 2026 / Accepted: 26 October 2026

Abstract
Cognitive dysfunction and cerebrovascular diseases are major public health challenges, yet the prognostic role
of cognitive impairment before a stroke in populations without stroke history remains unclear. We conducted
a community-based longitudinal study on 3,875 Chinese adults aged 40 and older who were free of stroke,
assessing their initial cognitive function with the Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE), where scores less
than 24 indicate impairment, and they were followed for 4.1 years. After adjusting for demographic factors,
vascular risks, homocysteine, and intracranial arterial stenosis, Cox proportional hazards models indicated
that cognitive impairment independently forecasted the occurrence of ischemic stroke [adjusted Hazard Ratio
(aHR) = 2.55, 95% Confidence Interval (CI): 1.33–4.87] and any stroke (aHR = 2.32, 95% CI:1.24–4.36), but
not all-cause mortality (aHR = 1.79, 95% CI:0.96–3.36). These findings indicate that baseline cognitive impair-
ment in stroke-free adults aged 40 and older is a significant predictor of future cerebrovascular events, empha-
sizing its importance in early risk assessment and prevention strategies for groups like Chinese adults who
have not experienced a stroke.

Introduction

Cognitive dysfunction and cerebrovascular diseases are increasingly recognized as significant global health chal-
lenges. In the United States, 22.7% of individuals over 65 are affected by mild cognitive impairment from all
causes. This prevalence is double that of Alzheimer’s disease, which is 11.3%, thereby imposing substantial soci-
etal costs1. Concurrently, the incidence of stroke in China increased by 86% from 1990 to 2019, reaching 276.7
cases per 100,000 population2. The intricate relationship between these conditions is highlighted by the fact that
cerebrovascular diseases significantly contribute to cognitive decline and dementia.
   Current studies mainly concentrate on cognitive impairment following a stroke, impacting 30–50% of those
who have experienced a stroke3. A cross-sectional study across multiple centers in China found that 78.7% of
patients with first-ever ischemic stroke developed post-stroke cognitive impairment (PSCI) when evaluated 3
to 6 months later4. The Berlin Manifesto also emphasizes that preventing stroke could significantly reduce the
incidence of dementia5. Furthermore, previous studies suggest that PSCI is associated with recurrent stroke and
increased mortality, mediated by vascular risk factors and neurodegeneration6. However, the predictive role of
baseline cognitive deficits in cerebrovascular events remains underexplored.
   Increasing evidence suggests that a slight decline in cognitive function may indicate subclinical vascular
issues, such as cerebral small vessel disease or systemic vascular dysfunction7. The presence of apolipoprotein E
(APOE)-ε4 and other genetic factors emphasize potential shared mechanisms between cognitive decline and the
risk of stroke8. Nevertheless, previous research, mainly involving Western groups9, has demonstrated inconsis-
tent associations between baseline cognitive impairment and stroke risk, with a hazard ratio (HR) of 1.19 reported
in meta-analyses10. These studies often failed to adequately adjust for vascular markers or socioeconomic con-
founders. Notably, no large-scale investigations have addressed this issue in Chinese populations, where stroke
etiology and risk profiles differ significantly from those in Western populations. These differences include a
higher prevalence of intracranial atherosclerosis (ICAS) and elevated homocysteine levels11,12. Therefore, this
study was performed to evaluate whether baseline cognitive impairment can independently predict incident cere-
brovascular events in a Chinese community-based cohort without a history of stroke, utilizing samples from the
Asymptomatic Polyvascular Abnormalities Community (APAC) study.


Materials and methods

Study design and population

The APAC study is a community-based, prospective, long-term follow-up investigation aimed at examining the
epidemiology of asymptomatic polyvascular abnormalities in Chinese adults. The study design has been pre-
viously detailed in the literature13. Briefly, the APAC study initially enrolled 5,440 participants aged 40 years

or older, who had no history of cardiovascular disease (CVD), transient ischemic attack (TIA), or stroke at
baseline, and exhibited no neurological deficits indicative of prior stroke. During 2010 and 2011, data on poten-
tial cardiovascular risk factors were collected, and all participants underwent transcranial Doppler sonography,
ankle-brachial index measurement, and bilateral carotid duplex sonography. In the first follow-up examination
conducted in 2012/2013, additional assessments, including cognitive function evaluation, were performed. The
present study further excluded individuals with documented stroke or CVD prior to the first follow-up. Ethical
approval was obtained from the Ethics Committees of Beijing Tiantan Hospital (Approval No: 2010-014-01) and
Kailuan General Hospital (Approval No: 2006-05), in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Informed
consent was obtained from all participants.

Assessment of cognitive function

Cognitive function was measured using the Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE) in the first follow-up exam-
ination from 2012 to 2013. The MMSE assesses orientation to time and place, attention, calculation and construc-
tion, learning, language, and memory, with a range of 0 to 3014. Higher scores indicate better performance. A
score of < 24 was defined as cognitive impairment in the present study15.

Association of covariates

Information on baseline characteristics such as age, gender, smoking status, alcohol consumption, physical activ-
ity, and education was obtained through standardized questionnaires conducted by trained individuals. Educa-
tional levels were categorized into three levels: illiteracy/primary school, middle school, and high school or
above. The classification of smoking and alcohol consumption included never, former, or current, while alcohol
use was further broken down into moderate, intermediate, heavy, unspecified quantity, or former use. Physical
activity was categorized as inactive (no exercise), moderately active (1–80 min per week), or very active (more
than 80 min per week). Hypertension was defined by a systolic/diastolic blood pressure of ≥ 140/90 mmHg, the
use of antihypertensive medication, or a self-reported history of hypertension. Diabetes was identified by a fast-
ing blood glucose level of ≥ 126 mg/dl, the use of glucose-lowering medications, or a self-reported diagnosis.
Dyslipidemia was defined by a self-reported history, current use of cholesterol lowering medicine, or a total
cholesterol level ≥ 220 mg/dl or triglyceride ≥ 150 mg/dl or low density lipoprotein ≥ 160 mg/dl13. Transcranial
Doppler ultrasound was used to assess ICAS, using established velocity thresholds. Carotid stenosis, defined as
≥ 50% stenosis, was evaluated using duplex ultrasound following the guidelines established by the Society of
Radiologists in Ultrasound16.

Follow-up and events

Participants in the study were observed through in-person interviews held every two years during regular medical
check-ups until December 31, 2015, the occurrence of a predefined final event, or death. These follow-ups were
conducted by physicians and nurses who were blinded to the baseline data. For those participants who missed
the follow-up exams, outcome data was gathered by reviewing death certificates from provincial vital statistics
offices, discharge summaries from the 11 hospitals, and medical records. The primary outcome was the first
incidence of stroke, diagnosed in accordance with the American Heart Association/American Stroke Association
(AHA/ASA) criteria17, confirmed by brain computed tomography (CT) or magnetic resonance imaging (MRI).
Strokes were divided into three primary categories: ischemic stroke, intracerebral hemorrhage, and subarachnoid
hemorrhage. The same diagnostic criteria were used in all the hospitals involved. In cases of fatal incidents or
when neuroimaging was unavailable, diagnosis was based on clinical evaluation and examination of medical
records following AHA/ASA guidelines.

Statistical analysis

All analyses were conducted using SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc.). A two-sided P < 0.05 was considered sta-
tistically significant. Normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test; variables with
non-normal distribution were summarized as median (IQR) and compared using the Kruskal-Wallis tests. Cat-
egorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages and were compared using the chi-square test.
To investigate the associations between baseline cognitive impairment and outcomes such as ischemic stroke,
any stroke (including ischemic stroke, intracerebral hemorrhage, and subarachnoid hemorrhage), and mortal-
ity, Cox proportional hazards models were employed, using participants with normal cognition as the reference
group. The proportional hazard assumption was met for survival analysis using the Schoenfeld residual plot
method (P > 0.05). Both unadjusted and adjusted hazard ratios (HRs), along with their 95% confidence intervals
(CIs), were computed. Three models were developed: Unadjusted Model; Model 1 was adjusted for age, gender,
and education; and Model 2 included additional adjustments for homocysteine levels, ICAS, hypertension, and
occupation. Covariates were selected based on their established association with stroke risk and their imbalance
between groups in baseline comparisons. The Kaplan-Meier method and log-rank test were utilized to estimate
the cumulative incidence of ischemic stroke, any stroke, and mortality.


Results

Study population and exclusions

The original APAC study had 5,440 participants. In this study, 53 participants with a recorded history of stroke or
CVD before the first follow-up, along with 1,512 people missing MMSE scores, were not included. As a result,
3,875 participants were enrolled in the final longitudinal cohort study to explore the link between initial cogni-
tive function and stroke risk (Fig. 1). Compared to the participants who were included, the excluded individuals
(n = 1,565) were older (mean age: 54.6 vs. 51.6 years, p < 0.0001), had a higher population of males (67.0% vs.
57.0%, p < 0.0001), and had higher prevalences of hypertension (53.4% vs. 45.8%, p < 0.0001), diabetes (14.8%
vs. 10.9%, p < 0.0001) and ICAS (15.7% vs. 12.2%) (Supplementary Table S1).

Basic characteristics of the studied population

A total of 3,875 community residents without strokes participated in the study, showing a median MMSE score
of 29 (IQR: 27–30). Among these participants, 192 individuals (5.0%) had cognitive impairment at baseline.
These participants were significantly older (median age: 62.8 vs. 51.3 years, P < 0.0001), had lower educational
attainment (48.4% vs. 8.2% with primary education or less, P < 0.0001), and were more likely to be manual
workers (51.8.% vs. 53.1%, P = 0.0005) than their cognitively normal counterparts. Additionally, they had higher
prevalence of hypertension (57.3% vs. 45.2%, P = 0.0010), elevated homocysteine levels (15.8 vs. 13.2 µmol/L,
P < 0.0001), and ICAS (24.0% vs. 11.5%, P < 0.0001) (Table 1).

Association between cognitive function and cerebrovascular events over a 4.1-year follow-up

Over a median follow-up of 4.1 years, the cumulative incidences of ischemic stroke, any stroke, and all-cause
mortality were 2.1%, 2.3%, and 2.2%, respectively. Kaplan–Meier curves showed significantly higher event rates
in participants with cognitive impairment across all outcomes (log-rank test P < 0.01 for each; Fig. 2). In fully
adjusted Cox models, cognitive impairment remained an independent predictor of ischemic stroke (aHR = 2.55,
95% CI: 1.33–4.87) and any stroke (aHR = 2.32, 95% CI: 1.24–4.36), but was not significantly associated with
all-cause mortality (aHR = 1.79, 95% CI: 0.96–3.36; Table 2).


Discussion

the MMSE, independently predicts cerebrovascular events in Chinese adults without a history of stroke. Over
a median follow-up of 4.1 years, individuals with cognitive impairment had a 2.32 to 2.55 times higher risk of
experiencing ischemic and any strokes than those with normal cognitive function. This association remained

that cognitive screening could be valuable for identifying stroke-free adults who are at a greater risk of experienc-
ing cerebrovascular events in the future.
   Our findings are consistent with emerging evidence that baseline cognitive impairment may reflect subclinical
vascular pathology. However, the effect sizes in our study (aHRs: 2.32–2.55 for cognitive impairment predicting
stroke occurrence) were significantly larger than the pooled HR of 1.19 from previous meta-analyses of mainly
Western groups10. This difference may be attributed to several factors. First, the higher prevalence of intracranial
atherosclerosis in Chinese populations18 may amplify the vascular burden associated with cognitive decline. Sec-
ond, unlike previous studies, we adjusted for homocysteine and ICAS, key vascular markers prevalent in Asian
populations, yet cognitive impairment remained an independent predictor, suggesting its potential role as an inte-
grative marker of diffuse vascular injury or neurovascular unit dysfunction beyond focal stenosis19.
   The link between cognitive function and cerebrovascular health is well documented. In a 10-year follow-up
of older Chinese adults, cognitive impairment predicted all-cause and cardiovascular mortality20. This associa-
tion extends to cerebrovascular events, including stroke21, and is further supported by neuroimaging evidence:
the Framingham Offspring Study reported that white matter hyperintensities and brain infarcts, both markers
of cerebrovascular disease, were associated with high risk of stroke and dementia22. Several mechanisms may
explain the elevated stroke risk observed in stroke-free individuals with cognitive impairment. On one hand,
traditional vascular risk factors, including hypertension, diabetes, and hyperlipidemia, are prevalent in this popu-
lation and may promote subclinical cerebrovascular damage such as silent brain lesions or microinfarcts. These
subclinical damage may initially be asymptomatic but increase the risk of clinical stroke. Empirical evidence

suggests an association between cognitive impairment and vascular alterations in the brain, potentially predispos-
ing individuals to ischemic events23,24. Beyond these conventional pathways, cognitive impairment itself might
reflect underlying cerebrovascular pathology, such as cerebral small vessel disease, characterized by white matter
changes and lacunar infarcts. These are associated with both cognitive decline and higher stroke risk, suggest-
ing that cognitive impairment may not only result from but also contribute to the progression of cerebrovascular
disease25,26. In addition to these vascular mechanisms, systemic immunometabolic dysregulation has emerged
as a complementary pathway linking cognitive impairment to cerebrovascular risk. Recent work found that the
neutrophil-to-HDL cholesterol ratio (NHR), a marker of peripheral inflammation and metabolic imbalance, was
nonlinearly associated with cognitive impairment in older adults27, suggesting that peripheral immuno-metabolic
status may reflect—and potentially contribute to—brain vulnerability to vascular events.
   Our study has several strengths, including its prospective design, community-based sample, and thorough
adjustment for ICAS, a critical confounder frequently neglected in stroke prediction research. Nonetheless, sev-
eral limitations should be noted. Firstly, cognitive function was assessed using the MMSE alone. Although prac-
tical for large-scale screening, this tool has known limitations, including potential educational bias and lower
sensitivity for detecting mild or domain-specific cognitive deficits. Although we adjusted for education, residual
confounding cannot be excluded. Secondly, about 28.8% of participants were excluded due to missing MMSE
data, and these individuals differed from those included in several baseline characteristics, which may have intro-
duced selection bias—though we have adjusted for these factors in our models. Thirdly, the study did not measure
certain socioeconomic factors (e.g., income, dietary habits) or other potential biological confounders, which may
contribute to residual confounding. Fourthly, the relative short follow-up period may have limited our ability to
detect long-term associations between cognitive trajectories, cerebrovascular outcomes, and mortality. The lack
of an association with all-cause mortality differs from results found in previous dementia research28, which may
be partly explained by this shorter follow-up and the relatively young baseline age (median: 53.4 years), where
competing risks are non-negligible. Finally, our findings may not be generalizable to non-Asian populations,
given the distinct stroke etiology in Chinese cohorts.
   In conclusion, our findings suggest that cognitive impairment is associated with an increased risk of future
cerebrovascular events in stroke-free Chinese adults. This may aid early risk identification in this population.
Further studies are needed to explore whether vascular risk reduction or cognitive monitoring could improve
stroke prediction.
