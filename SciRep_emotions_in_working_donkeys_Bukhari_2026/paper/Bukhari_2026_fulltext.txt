Abstract
     Quantitative measures of emotion play a crucial role in objectively assessing animal welfare. However,
there is currently no research available on donkey emotions. Our study investigated spontaneous blink

rate (SBR), eye temperature (ET), and heart rate variability (HRV), quantified as RMSSD (root mean square
of the successive differences between beat-to-beat intervals), as potential measures of donkey emotions.

                                   L E
Thirty-four donkeys were exposed to three treatments: positive, negative, and control. The positive and

negative treatments were feeding high-quality lucerne and exposing donkeys to the sound of hair clippers,


                              T I
respectively. The control group was recorded standing in their usual environment, and were not subjected
to any additional positive or negative stimuli. Data were analyzed using linear models. SBR increased

                           A R
during positive treatment and decreased with the negative treatment compared to the control. An
increase in ET was found for both negative and positive treatment compared to the control. We found a
positive relationship between changes in SBR and HRV (estimated=1.32, t=5.44, p<0.001), whereas
negative relationships were found between changes in ET and HRV (estimated= -12.76, t= -5.93, p<0.001).
ET, SBR, and HRV may act as indicators of emotions in donkeys. Additional studies should investigate these
metrics through a thorough analysis of donkey behavior.
Keywords: animal sentience, cardiology, physiology, thermography, welfare, working equids
    1. Introduction

    Animal emotion is a complex response comprising subjective, physiological, neural, and cognitive
components to a stimulus or event 1,2. Emotions play a crucial role as they facilitate the selection of
appropriate behavioral responses to external and internal events 3. Emotions have two main dimensions:
arousal (calm versus excited) and valence (negative or positive) 4,5. The existence and nature of emotions
in non-human animals are believed to be correlated with those in humans and to have evolved from
similar mechanisms 6. However, emotions are subjective internal experiences that cannot be easily
measured 7. Consequently, techniques for understanding and assessing animal emotions, particularly
positive ones, are limited 3. Currently, the focus lies in inferring emotions by analyzing behavioral,

                                      ARTICLEMANUSCRIPT
                                              IN PRESS



physiological, and neurobiological indicators 7,8. Animal emotions change in response to positive and
negative stimuli 9,10. Therefore, accurately inferring animal emotional states from behavioral and
physiological evidence is important for welfare, because emotions reflect how animals experience their
lives and influence how they respond to environmental challenges and resources 11,12. There has been an
increasing interest in identifying non-invasive and reliable indicators of both positive and negative
emotions in animals 9,10,13.
     Globally, there are around 50.5 million donkeys (Equus asinus) 14, and they support approximately 600
million people, especially in low and middle-income countries (LMICs) where they are relied upon for
agriculture, construction, and transportation of goods 15,16. Despite their importance, relatively little
research has been conducted to assess and improve the welfare of working donkeys in LMICs 17. This has
led to their neglect in government-level animal welfare regulations 18, although working equids were
recently included in the World Organization for Animal Health’s (WOAH) Terrestrial Animal Welfare Code
19. Welfare challenges for working donkeys in LMICs are multifactorial, driven by interacting

socioeconomic and structural factors (poverty, limited access to education and veterinary care, reliance
on donkeys for livelihoods, and inadequate legal or welfare protections). Together, these factors can lead
to harsh working conditions and harm donkey health and welfare 12. This is particularly evident in donkeys
used for carrying heavy loads when owners may overlook their emotions and pain 20.


     Spontaneous blink rate (SBR) has been utilized to measure stress responses in humans 21 and offers a

non-invasive method for assessing stress in animals 13,22,23. Blinks serve multiple functions, including


                                                                  R E
corneal lubrication and the resetting of eye movements during fixation error correction 24. The association
between SBR and stress appears to be influenced by dopaminergic activity. In monkeys, SBR increased

following subcutaneous administration of dopamine agonists and was reduced with dopamine


antagonists 25. Humans demonstrate a decrease in SBR during periods of focused attention and cognitive
effort 21. SBR is a reliable measure of stress in humans and animals 10,13,21. The use of SBR to assess stress


                                   L E
or negative emotional state is promising, as it is a non-invasive and inexpensive measure of stress 10. Once
investigated and standardized under donkey working conditions, it could help donkey handlers, owners,

and veterinarians to make better decisions for their care and ultimately improve the welfare of animals.


     Eye temperature (ET) measurement effectively evaluates animal stress across a range of species 26–29.
This method involves using infrared thermography to assess the temperature of the eye, usually at the
medial canthus, which can reflect the physiological and emotional state of horses (Equus caballus) 30, goats
(Capra hircus) 31, sheep (Ovis aries) 32, cats (Felis catus) 33, dogs (Canis familiaris) 34, and cattle (Bos taurus)
35. Changes in eye temperature can be influenced by the proximity of the orbit to the brain and changes

in blood flow caused by vascular activity, which are commonly affected by stress 36. A positive relationship
between eye temperature (ET) and salivary cortisol levels has been reported 29. Moreover, a negative
relationship between ET and heart rate variability (HRV) was observed in response to stress 26. The use of
ET as a non-invasive measure of stress has great potential for practical application. However, there is
currently no research available on how the ET of donkeys varies during positive and negative emotional
states.

     HRV is the physiological phenomenon of variations in the time interval between heartbeats 3,26 and
can be measured as root mean square of the successive differences of the beat-to-beat interval, recorded
in milliseconds (RMSSD) 10. It represents the ability of the heart to respond to a variety of physiological
and environmental stimuli 37. RMSSD is a valuable tool for assessing animal stress and positive emotional
states 3,10. The interplay between the sympathetic and parasympathetic branches of the nervous system
influences the HRV 38,39 and controls the physiological responses of animals to positive and negative stimuli

40–43. Animals in a relaxed and comfortable state exhibit increased RMSSD 40,44, whereas those experiencing

stressful situations exhibit decreased HRV 26,45,46. This decrease in RMSSD indicates an increase in
sympathetic activity and a decrease in parasympathetic influence, reflecting a state of heightened stress
10,26,45,46. Moreover, it is reported that animals engaging in positive interactions exhibit increased RMSSD
40,44. This suggests a higher parasympathetic influence and a state of relaxation and contentment 40.

However, RMSSD should be interpreted cautiously because it is influenced by the specific metric used,
heart rate, respiration, physical activity, and the level of physiological arousal. While RMSSD is a promising
measure of stress and positive emotions in animals (e.g., goats 3,38, horses 10,47), donkeys have not yet
been investigated in this regard.

    Donkeys are known for their stoic behavior 48,49 and typically exhibit a reduced flight response and
tend to show freeze behaviors more often than flight behaviors when compared to horses 48,49. Many
people believe that donkeys feel less pain and stress because they hide signs of pain, making it difficult to
assess their welfare 50. However, this perception is due to a lack of understanding of donkeys and their
emotional states, which can greatly affect their welfare. Recent research has shown that recognizing
donkey emotions and feelings of pain (i.e., recognizing that donkeys are sentient) is associated with
improved management practices 20. Therefore, it is necessary to investigate the indicators of positive and
negative emotions in donkeys to understand and protect their welfare.

treatment exposure in donkeys. We hypothesized that positive treatment would lead to a greater change

in SBR than negative treatment. In contrast, negative treatment would result in a greater change in ET

than positive treatment, and the change in HRV would be greater during positive treatment than during
negative treatment.

    3   Discussion

           Quantitative measures of emotion are essential for evaluating animal emotions and welfare.
However, there is still a very long way to go to make accurate inferences about physiology and
emotional state in animals 51. In our study, we used SBR, ET, and HRV to measure donkeys’ physiological
responses to positive and negative stimuli. We tested donkeys with positive and negative treatments and
measured their response in terms of SBR, ET, and HRV. Our results showed an increase in ET and a
decrease in SBR in response to a negative treatment (clipper sounds), which is similar to previous studies
in horses 10,13,40,41. However, both ET and SBR increased in response to the positive treatment (provision
of high-quality lucerne). When we calculated the change in each parameter (control minus positive and
control minus negative treatment), the direction of change in each variable became more obvious. In
response to positive treatment, the change in HRV and SBR was higher, but the change in ET was lower,
while during negative treatment, the change in HRV and SBR was lower, and the change in ET was higher.
This suggests that these measures capture different aspects of the emotional response. HRV and SBR are
likely more related to autonomic regulation, attention, and emotional valence, whereas ET mainly reflects
arousal or sympathetic activation. Thus, ET may rise during both positive and negative high-arousal events,
with larger increases in negative or threatening situations. These findings suggest that SBR, ET, and HRV
are promising measures of stress and positive emotions in donkeys, similar to humans 21, horses 10,26, dogs
9,34,45, goats 31,44, and cattle 35. We propose that instead of considering absolute values for ET, SBR, and




                                                                  S S
HRV, we should assess donkey emotions using the change in ET, SBR, and HRV.



                                                               R E
         Although donkeys may appear behaviorally stoic and show limited outward signs of their
emotional state, this does not indicate reduced emotional or physiological sensitivity. Our findings show
that donkeys exhibited measurable physiological responses to both positive and negative treatments in


terms of SBR, ET, and HRV, which are similar to those of horses 10,13,28, dogs 9,34,45, humans 21, goats 31,44,
sheep 32, cats 33, and cattle 35,46. Donkeys are known for their stoicism and display a reduced flight response


                                  L E
and increased freeze behaviors compared to horses 48,49. It is often believed that donkeys are stoic and

feel less pain or stress than horses. This perception is supported by their tendency to hide signs of pain,


                             T I
making it difficult to assess their well-being 50. However, this is due to a lack of understanding of donkeys
and their emotional states. Our study suggests that donkeys are not less sensitive than other animals,


                          A R
although they may show less overt behavioral change 52. Moreover, we know that owner recognition of
donkey emotions and feelings of pain benefits their welfare via improved management practices 20.
Furthermore, as donkeys may show few outward signs of stress, owners should not assume a quiet donkey
is unaffected. Instead, they should consider whether factors such as loud noises, unfamiliar equipment,
rough handling, isolation, or abrupt routine changes could be stressful even without obvious behavioral
responses. Awareness of these subtle physiological changes can help owners detect stress sooner and
adjust handling or management to improve welfare. Therefore, a better understanding of physiological
indicators of donkey emotions and subsequent improved education of donkey owners may help improve
their welfare in a challenging working environment.
         We found decreased SBR in response to negative treatment (a stressful situation). This is similar
to previous studies in horses 10,13,53 and humans 21. A possible explanation for the increase in SBR during
the positive treatment is that SBR is influenced by dopaminergic activity 54. In monkeys (Macaca
fascicularis), dopamine agonists increased SBR, whereas dopamine antagonists reduced SBR 25. However,
the present study did not measure dopamine, so this mechanism should be interpreted cautiously. Horses
appear to respond in a similar way. Crib-biting is a common stereotypy in horses and is associated with
stress, and crib-biting horses demonstrated lower SBR (15 to 11 blinks per min) than control animals.
Additionally, a decrease in SBR was observed in horses during the initial one-minute treatment (clipping)
compared to baseline (11 to 6 blinks per minute) 10. This suggests that the reduced SBR may be the
                                  ACCEPTED
                                    ARTICLEMANUSCRIPT
                                            IN PRESS



characteristic of a fight or flight response and increased sympathetic nervous system activity, allowing
horses to visually fixate on the stimulus before responding accordingly 10. In this study with horses 10, after
an initial decrease in SBR in response to the negative stimulus, SBR started increasing. This was not
observed in our study with donkeys. This may be because the horse researchers recorded SBR for an
extended period of time (10 minutes), allowing the horses to become habituated to clipper sounds.
Alternatively, there may be a difference between horses and donkeys in this regard. In comparison, during
a startle test with horses involving tossing a ball, SBR decreased (26 to 13 blinks) compared to the control
13. Our study demonstrates that SBR decreases in response to a stressful or startling situation.

Furthermore, human research has shown a reduction in SBR during periods of attention and cognitive
efforts 21. Potentially threatening stimuli may require cognitive effort to verify them as a threat, thus
reducing SBR. However, further investigation of changes in SBR over varying time spans is needed to
identify patterns in response to stressful or startling stimuli in donkeys.
         We found increased ET in response to negative treatments (a stress situation) similar to previous
studies in horses 26–29. Change in ET during positive experiences has yet to be investigated in horses,
although it has been assessed in other species. Goats' eye temperature increased from 36.8 to 37.9 °C
during a 1-minute stress situation caused by a person making noise and waving their arms in the animal
pen. However, dogs showed an increase in ET (36.7 to 37.3 °C) during feeding (a high arousal, positive
event) as compared to the control 9. We know that high arousal situations, irrespective of whether they


                                                                  S S
are positive or negative, can result in increased ocular vasodilation, leading to higher ET 9,30,55.
Interestingly, the change in ET during the negative treatment (1.22±0.57°C) was greater than positive


                                                               R E
treatment (0.81±0.56°C) in our study. Therefore, we propose that absolute ET is a measure of arousal,

whereas change in ET is the measure of positive and negative emotions in donkeys. Since negative
emotions are implicated in ‘fight-or-flight’ responses to threatening stimuli, we might expect quicker

mobilization of the physiological responses underlying temperature changes to negative versus positive
contexts 56. Ultimately, the magnitude and time course of emotionally induced changes in ET may offer a

                                  L E
means to examine emotional responses in relation to arousal and valence. Moreover, in agreement with

our study, ET was higher (35.9 to 36.8 °C) after the novel object test (fear test) compared to basal ET in

                             T I
horses 28. An increase in ET was also seen in response to a number of stressful conditions, for example,

clipping (an increase of 3.5°C) 55, and with an over-tightened noseband (an increase of between 0.44 and

1.8 °C) in horses 26,57. It is suggested that an increase in ET was caused by increased blood flow to the eyes
as a sympathetic nervous system response 36. Furthermore, an increase in ET was also positively correlated
to salivary cortisol in response to stress in horses 29. Therefore, ET should be interpreted as an indicator
of physiological arousal or autonomic activation. In potentially negative contexts, changes in ET may help
assess stress-system activation when interpreted alongside other physiological and behavioral measures.
         We found an increase in RMSSD in response to positive treatment and a reduction in HRV in
response to negative treatment (stressful situation), which is consistent with previous studies in horses
40–43. HRV is a sensitive measure of both physical and emotional stress in horses 58, and it is a commonly

used autonomic nervous system (ANS) correlate, estimating the sympathovagal balance as a
psychophysiological marker of emotion regulation across species. It is a sensitive measure of both physical
and emotional stress in horses 58, and it is a commonly used autonomic nervous system (ANS) correlate,
estimating the sympathovagal balance as a psychophysiological marker of emotion regulation across
species 40. RMSSD was higher in response to positive calls (65ms) compared to negative ones (45ms) in
goats 44. Similarly, RMSSD can also vary seasonally as Przewalski’s horses (Equus ferus przewalskii)
exhibited low RMSSD (70 ms) during winter, when resources are limited, indicating decreased metabolic
rates coupled with stress, compared with summer (RMSSD = 250 ms) 41. Furthermore, horses showed a
decrease in RMSSD (46.3 to 15.8ms) during novel object and handling tests 42, indicating a shift of the
autonomic balance towards a sympathetic dominance. The RMSSD decreased (76 to 46 ms) in response

to training of young sport horses, and the lowest values were reached during mounting of a rider (23 ms)
43. The most pronounced stress reaction occurred in response to the horses being mounted by a rider, a

situation resembling an imminent threat under natural conditions. Furthermore, in horses, RMSSD
remains high in interaction with familiar handlers as compared to unfamiliar handlers 40, similar to the
change in RMSSD, which was high in donkeys when lucerne was provided for eating. Therefore, RMSSD is
a valuable measure to quantify sympathovagal balance, which allows a more precise assessment of stress
and positive emotions 40.
       To our knowledge, this is the first report examining how SBR, ET, and HRV vary in donkeys exposed
to stimuli intended to induce positive and negative emotional states. SBR is a relatively easy measure to
examine, although the observer must be fairly close to the animal in question 10,13. Monitoring changes in
SBR can alert the observer to changes in the donkey’s emotional state. A decrease in SBR may indicate a
stressful situation for the donkey. This is particularly significant in low-resource settings where access to
advanced technology may be limited. ET and HRV are important measures of the emotional state of a
donkey, but they need very sophisticated and expensive equipment for monitoring. Moreover, ET should
be interpreted carefully as it increases in both positive and negative treatment 9,55. It is important to note
that ET, SBR, and RMSSD may serve as measures of donkey emotions, but require context. SBR may be
relevant to affective processes; its interpretation across positive, negative, and neutral contexts remains
complex and requires further investigation. RMSSD was the only HRV metric analyzed in this study. Future


                                                                 S S
studies should include a broader HR/HRV analysis, including heart rate, additional time-domain measures,
frequency-domain indices, and non-linear measures, while carefully accounting for artefacts, respiratory


                                                              R E
influences, and ectopic beats. It would also be helpful if further research could include longer observations

of donkeys during positive and negative treatment and after removal of the treatment, as the interplay
between SBR, ET, and HRV in this period may reveal more about the importance of these measures for

the assessment of stress and positive affective state. Future research should include longer observation
periods during both positive and negative treatments, along with a washout period. Examining the

                                  L E
interplay between SBR, ET, and RMSSD in this phase may clarify how useful these measures are for

assessing stress and positive affective state. In relation to this, future studies should examine donkey

                             T I
behaviors, such as reluctance to move forward, ear position, and muzzle and nasal tension, as this study

only included one behavior (SBR) and lacked a comprehensive behavioral investigation during treatments.

As interest grows, it is important to develop a detailed understanding of their potential and limitations.
        4 Conclusion
       Our study showed that ET, SBR, and RMSSD varied in working donkeys exposed to positive and
negative treatment. The positive treatment was associated with numerically greater changes in RMSSD
and SBR, and a smaller change in ET. In contrast, the negative treatment was associated with numerically
smaller changes in RMSSD and SBR, and a larger change in ET. Importantly, SBR may provide a simple and
practical behavioral indicator of donkey responses to positive and negative treatment, without the
requirement for sophisticated equipment. This is particularly significant in low-resource settings where
access to advanced technology may be limited. Furthermore, our study challenges the notion that
donkeys are stoic and experience less pain or stress than horses. We found that donkeys respond to
positive and negative treatments in a similar way to other species such as horses, goats, dogs, and cattle.

5.2 Sample size estimation
         The sample size was estimated using G*Power 3.1 software 59. Previous research reported that
SBR decreases significantly (11.1 to 6.2 blinks per minute) under minor stress (negative) situations in
horses 10. Conversely, ET increases significantly (32.3 to 35.9 °C) under acute stress (negative) situations
as compared to control situations in horses 29. These studies were used to support the biological
plausibility of detecting treatment-related changes in donkeys, but not to derive the effect size for the
power analysis. We hypothesized that changes to the SBR, ET, and HRV of donkeys exposed to positive
and negative stimuli would be comparable to previous horse studies 10,29. Therefore, the input parameters
set for G*Power were alpha (α = 0.05), power (0.95), and effect size (ρ = 0.5), leading to a sample size
estimation of 34 donkeys for the current research.
5.3 Animals
        Thirty-four Pakistani working donkeys (age 6.05±0.31 years; Jennies 20, Jacks 14) were used for
the study. All the donkeys were working animals and were therefore accustomed to human presence.
They all remained in their home stable during the experiment and were housed individually. Donkeys
were physically fit with a good body condition score (2.88±0.40 out of 5) according to The Donkey
Sanctuary guidelines 60. Donkeys were assessed by an equine veterinary surgeon at rest for overall health
status based on The Brooke's SEBWAT welfare assessment tool 15. Healthy donkeys were recruited based


                                                                S S
on guidelines recommended from previous research 15. Verbal consent was obtained from the donkey

owners prior to the commencement of data collection. Owners reported that none of the donkeys were

given any medication during the testing period or for one month before data collection.
5.4 Study design and data collection
         A randomized crossover trial was used to assess physiological responses to three treatments, with

a one-hour washout period. Prior to undertaking the experiments, the researcher (SSUHB) habituated


                               C L
each donkey to his presence. This was achieved by spending two hours daily with the donkeys for one and

a half months to minimize any effect of an unfamiliar person on the animals’ physiological responses. The


researcher also visited the donkeys with the relevant research equipment to habituate them to both the
equipment and the researcher. We chose the SBR as the habituation criterion. When we noticed that the

blink rate (blinks per min) stayed constant in the presence and absence of the experimenter and research
equipment, we concluded that habituation had been achieved. Following habituation, donkeys were
subject to three treatments: positive, negative, and control. The positive treatment was the provision of
high-quality green lucerne (food, 8-liter bucket). Donkeys in this area like fresh lucerne, but they usually
don’t have access to it because it’s expensive. The negative treatment was the sound of hair clippers held
close to the donkey’s right ear. Positive and negative treatments were chosen based on previous research
in dogs 9 and horses 10. Donkeys standing calmly in their usual living environment without positive or
negative treatment and any other interaction were used as the control. On data collection day, donkeys
were not working to prevent confounding effects from working conditions. Each donkey was used as its
own control to evaluate the relationship between variables within each treatment period. Data on SBR,
ET, and HRV were collected for each donkey during each treatment. The treatment sequence was
randomly assigned by generating random numbers between one to three using Microsoft Excel. Number
one was designated as the control treatment, number two as the positive treatment, and number three
as the negative treatment. As the trials followed a randomized crossover design, each donkey received
the treatments (control, positive, and negative) in a random order on the same day (single session). All
measurements were taken between 10:00 and 14:00 each day to avoid any confounding effects of diurnal
variation in physiological recordings. Data were collected during each treatment for three minutes 13. This

5.5 Spontaneous blink rate
        The donkey's SBR was recorded by filming its eyes using a GoPro HERO Black camera at 30 frames
per second. The videos were transferred to a laptop and analyzed for SBR, similar to a previous research
10. Measurements of SBR were made using coding of the offline videos by two coders (coder-1 and coder-

2). Each coder counted the number of blinks per minute. SBR was reported as blinks per minute for each
donkey under each test condition.
5.6 Eye temperature
        A standardized infrared thermography protocol for ET (°C) was used 61. Each donkey was filmed
under the shade using a thermographic camera (FLIR T530, Teledyne FLIR Systems Inc., Wilsonville, USA),
placed at 90° to the sagittal plane of the eye and at a distance of one meter 61. The left eye was selected
following the experimental protocol adopted for horses 10. The maximum temperature at the medial
canthus of the eye was recorded using FLIR Tools+ software, as it gives the most reliable temperature
measurement 32. The camera's distance from the donkey was measured using laser technology fitted
within the FLIR T530 camera. Thermographic videos were transferred to a laptop via SD cards. The data
were extracted from videos using FLIR-Tools+ software. The maximum temperature at the medial canthus
of the eye was recorded as it gives the most reliable temperature measurement 32. The ambient
temperature and humidity were measured using a portable weather station (LondiSun LS-204), and wind
speed was measured using a Kestrel-1000 digital anemometer, as they may affect thermography in field
conditions.

5.7 Heart rate variability
         Each donkey was fitted with a heart rate monitor (Polar Equine V800) to record beat-to-beat
interval (R-R interval). HRV describes the variation in the time intervals between successive heartbeats. In
this study, HRV was quantified using the root mean square of the successive differences between beat-
to-beat intervals (RMSSD), a time-domain HRV metric reported in milliseconds 10. The data collected from
the Polar Equine V800 were exported to the laptop using the Polar FlowSync software. The data were
analyzed using Kubios software (Kubios HRV Standard version 3.5.0, Kubios Oy, Kuopio, Finland), with a
low threshold (0.35 s) for artifact correction according to previous research 10,23.
5.8 Statistical analysis
         All the variables are presented as mean and standard deviation (mean ± SD). The Shapiro-Wilk
test was used to test the normality of the data. SBR data extracted by coder-1 were used for the main
statistical analysis because coder-1 was the primary trained observer who coded all videos. Coder-2
independently coded the videos to assess inter-observer agreement. The use of coder-1 data was
considered appropriate because Bland–Altman analysis showed high agreement between coders, with
minimal bias and narrow limits of agreement across the control, positive, and negative treatments. For
SBR, Band-Altman plots were used to determine the level of agreement between coders (C1 and C2) and
bias. Data were normally distributed according to the Shapiro–Wilk test (p > 0.05); therefore, raw,


                                                                S S
untransformed data were used for all analyses. Linear mixed-effects models (with an individual donkey as

a random effect) were fitted to individually compare SBR, ET, and HRV (quantified as RMSSD) under the

positive or negative treatment with the control. The order of the treatment application was included in

the model as a fixed effect to account for the randomized crossover nature of the study. The change in
each parameter (SBR, ET, and HRV) was calculated (control minus positive and control minus negative

treatment). The changes in SBR and ET were compared with an established physiological indicator of

autonomic nervous system activity associated with stress and emotional arousal (change in HRV 10) in


                                   C L
animals using two repeated-measure linear regression models. An individual donkey was included as a
random effect. In the first model, change in HRV, quantified as RMSSD, was the outcome variable, with
